Your mate wants to continue the Valheim world while you are out. The usual answer is: rent a server, pay monthly, and hope nobody installs a cursed mod at 2am. But **save sharing vs dedicated servers** is not really a question of which option is more serious. It is a question of what your group actually does when nobody is looking.

If you play together occasionally, take turns hosting, and mostly want the world to survive bad decisions, a dedicated server can be a pricey way to solve the wrong problem. If people need to log in at random hours, build simultaneously, or keep a persistent community running, save sharing will not magically become a server. Different jobs. Different pain. No need to pretend otherwise.

## What a dedicated server actually buys you

A dedicated server runs the game world independently of any one player's PC. In the right game, that means the world stays online even when the usual host is asleep, at work, or pretending they did not see the group chat.

That is valuable when your crew has overlapping schedules and wants true multiplayer access. A Minecraft realm-style community, a busy Palworld group, or a modded server with players popping in throughout the week needs a shared machine running the world. People can connect at the same time, interact in real time, and keep the base moving forward without waiting for one person to hand anything over.

The catch is that persistence comes with overhead. You may pay a recurring hosting bill, or run hardware yourself and become the unpaid night-shift sysadmin. Then there are updates, mod compatibility, configuration files, performance limits, restarts, admin permissions, and backups. Even a well-run server can have a spectacularly bad evening when a mod update decides your carefully built factory is now archaeology.

A server is also not a backup plan merely because it is a server. It can corrupt saves. An admin can delete the wrong folder. A bad plugin can chew through a world. If your provider only keeps limited backups, or you never check them until disaster arrives, your always-on world is still one bad session away from becoming a very expensive memory.

## What save sharing is built to do

Save sharing is for games where one player hosts locally, but the group wants the world to belong to the group instead of being trapped on one person's SSD.

Think [Stardew Valley](https://checkpoint64.com/blog/stardew-valley-co-op-save-share/) farms, Factorio saves, Satisfactory factories, emulator memory cards, or a modded campaign that only needs one person playing at a time. Rather than paying to keep a server online every hour of the month, the save moves between trusted players. Whoever has the world checked out can play it. When they finish, the updated save goes back into the shared history for the next person.

The best version of this setup has rules enforced by software, not hopeful messages in Discord. A lock prevents two people from opening separate copies and creating a save-file custody battle. A shared logbook shows who took the world, what changed, and when it came back. Expiring locks stop a vanished friend from holding the save hostage forever because they went on holiday and forgot their PC was asleep.

That is the basic difference: a dedicated server gives everyone concurrent access; save sharing gives a group safe, orderly handoff. One is a live venue. The other is a protected cartridge being passed around without someone sitting on it.

## Save sharing vs dedicated servers: compare the real cost

The monthly price is the obvious bit. A hosted dedicated server charges whether your friends play forty hours this month or abandon the world after one dramatic weekend. Self-hosting avoids that subscription, but your electricity, hardware, broadband, and time do not appear from a loot chest.

Save sharing is usually cheaper for an asynchronous group because it does not need an always-on machine. You are storing and coordinating the save, not hiring a permanent home for a game process. Checkpoint64, for example, has a free plan that is actually free, then one-time upgrades for more storage and crew features. No subscription quietly nibbling at the budget while everyone is playing something else.

But cheaper is not automatically better. If four people want to play together every evening, forcing them into turns is not frugal. It is just annoying. A server earns its keep when it removes waiting, supports concurrent play, and stays available often enough to justify its running costs.

There is a less visible cost too: recovery. A dedicated server without dependable version history can turn a single broken update into a full-scale rescue operation. With save sharing and [versioned backups](https://checkpoint64.com/blog/game-save-version-history/), rolling back before the dodgy mod install or accidental overwrite should be a quick choice, not an evening spent inspecting folders with names like \`world\_final\_FINAL2\`.

## When save sharing wins

Save sharing is the cleaner choice when your group plays a world in shifts rather than all at once. Maybe one friend does the farming during lunch, another expands the base after work, and the person who understands conveyor belts is only allowed near the factory at weekends. Nobody needs a 24/7 server for that.

It also suits games that are fundamentally local-hosted or awkward to run as dedicated instances. Some co-op games tie progress, mods, or host behaviour closely to local save files. In those cases, trying to turn every session into server infrastructure can create more work than it saves.

Version history is especially useful for modded games and emulators. You can try a new modpack, an experimental patch, or a questionable save edit knowing there is a known-good point behind you. That is not cowardice. That is learning from the time a 200-hour base became unreadable after “just one small mod”.

Save sharing also gives host-dependent worlds an exit route. The group does not need to wait for the original host to be online. The save is shared, handoffs are controlled, and the world is no longer effectively owned by whoever happened to click New Game first.

### The limits are real

Save sharing is not simultaneous multiplayer. If the game requires the host world to be online for others to join, only the person currently holding the save can host that session. You cannot hand off a Satisfactory save at 6pm and expect three friends to keep building in it independently at 6:05pm. That is how parallel universes start, and neither one has the correct conveyor layout.

It also relies on everyone respecting the workflow. Good tools can enforce locks and keep versions, but they cannot stop someone from manually copying an old save into the wrong folder if they are determined to become the villain of the campaign.

## When a dedicated server is worth it

Choose a dedicated server when persistent, simultaneous access is the point. Your group may have players in different time zones, a community that drops in unpredictably, or a build that benefits from being live all day. Servers are made for that.

They also make sense when the game has mature server support and your crew is happy to manage it. If someone enjoys tuning settings, handling mods, and sorting permissions, great. Every party needs a person who reads the config documentation for fun. Let them cook.

Just do not confuse hosting with protection. Keep separate backups with proper version history, test restores before a crisis, and know where the actual world data lives. A rented server can be your multiplayer home without being the only copy of your progress.

## The practical answer: use the setup your sessions deserve

For many groups, the best answer is not ideological. Use a dedicated server for the game you play together live every week. Use save sharing for the campaign, farm, factory, or retro collection that people pick up in turns. Keep version history for both, because no hosting model can protect you from a [corrupted save](https://checkpoint64.com/blog/how-to-restore-corrupted-save-files-fast/), an accidental overwrite, or Dave experimenting with mods after two pints.

Before spending money, ask three boring but useful questions: Do we need to play at the same time? Does the world need to stay online when nobody is hosting? Will we play enough to justify a recurring bill? If the answer is no, no, and probably not, shared saves may be the much saner move.

Your world does not need enterprise infrastructure. It needs to be available when the right person wants to play, protected when somebody breaks it, and never stranded on one mate's PC.
